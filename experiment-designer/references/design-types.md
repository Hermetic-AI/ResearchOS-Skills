# Design Types & Selection Guide

Read this when choosing or reviewing an experimental design (workflow step 2). Start from the research question and the structure of the experimental units — never from a favorite design.

## The three principles behind every good design (Fisher)

- **Randomization** — assign treatments at random so confounders, known and unknown, are balanced in expectation. This is what turns a comparison into a causal claim.
- **Replication** — independent repetition **at the level the treatment is applied**, so variability is estimable. See pseudoreplication below.
- **Blocking / local control** — group similar units (batch, day, site, litter, plate) and randomize within blocks, removing that nuisance variation from the error term.

## Choosing a design type

```
What are you trying to learn?
│
├─ Compare a few predefined conditions (A vs B vs C)?
│   ├─ No known nuisance factor, units interchangeable
│   │     → COMPLETELY RANDOMIZED DESIGN (CRD)
│   ├─ A known nuisance factor (day, batch, site, litter, baseline score)
│   │     → RANDOMIZED BLOCK DESIGN (RBD): block on it, randomize within blocks
│   └─ You can only randomize groups, not individuals (classrooms, clinics)
│         → Cluster randomization — the CLUSTER is the unit of replication
│
├─ Quantify main effects AND interactions among a few factors (2–5)?
│     → FULL FACTORIAL (2^k): every combination of levels
│
└─ Screen MANY factors (5+) cheaply to find the few that matter?
      → FRACTIONAL FACTORIAL (2^(k−p)): main effects at the cost of
        aliasing them with interactions
```

### Completely randomized design (CRD)

Every unit independently assigned to a condition. Simplest and most flexible; best when units are homogeneous and no nuisance factor exists. With small n, pure coin-flip assignment can drift out of balance — use block randomization (permuted blocks) to guarantee balance throughout enrollment.

### Randomized block design (RBD)

Block units by a known nuisance factor, randomize all treatments within each block. The nuisance variation is removed from the error term instead of inflating it. Rule of thumb: *block what you can, randomize what you can't*. Always include the block factor in the analysis model.

### Full factorial (2^k)

Run every combination of factor levels: k two-level factors → 2^k runs. Estimates all main effects and all interactions. Cost explodes with k (2^6 = 64 runs), so use it for a handful of factors you actually care about.

### Fractional factorial (2^(k−p))

Run a carefully chosen fraction — e.g. 4 factors in 8 runs instead of 16. Generated by aliasing new factors with interactions of base factors (e.g. generator `d = ab`). **Cost: aliasing.** Main effects become confounded with interactions; know your alias structure before concluding a factor "has no effect". Use for screening, then follow up important factors with a fuller design.

### Response-surface designs (Box-Behnken, central composite)

Two-level factorials cannot detect curvature — if the goal is OPTIMIZATION (find the best factor settings, not just screen them), you need ≥ 3 levels. Box-Behnken runs each factor pair at its corners with others at center (no run with all factors at extremes — good when extreme×extreme conditions are unsafe/impossible); a central composite design (CCD) augments a 2^k core with axial "star" points. Both fit a quadratic model. Generate with `scripts/doe_designs.py --design boxbehnken|ccd`.

### Latin square

One treatment factor with g levels, blocked simultaneously on TWO nuisance factors (rows and columns, g levels each) — e.g. g subjects × g measurement periods, or g plates × g positions. Each treatment appears exactly once per row and column. **Assumption: no interactions** among treatment, row, and column; if interactions are plausible, use a factorial instead. Cost is only g² runs. Generate with `scripts/doe_designs.py --design latin` (rows, columns, and the treatment↔symbol mapping are all shuffled with the seed).

### Crossover design

Every subject receives MULTIPLE treatments in sequence (each serves as its own control) — the standard within-subject design for chronic/stable conditions. Threats: **carryover** (treatment A's effect persists into period 2 — insert a washout period) and **period/order effects** (fatigue, learning, drift — balance the order across subjects, e.g. AB vs BA, or a Latin square for >2 treatments). Analyze with period and sequence terms in the model; if carryover is detected, only period-1 data is cleanly interpretable.

### Split-plot design

For factorial experiments where one factor is HARD TO CHANGE (oven temperature, field irrigation, model pretraining) and another is easy (substrate, subplot crop, fine-tuning setting): randomize the hard factor at the whole-plot level (few resets), then randomize the easy factor within each whole plot. **The analysis pitfall: whole-plot and sub-plot factors have DIFFERENT error terms and different effective replication** — the hard factor is replicated only as many times as whole plots, so its power is low even when the total run count looks large. Handing split-plot data to a plain factorial ANOVA overstates significance of the whole-plot factor.

### Within-subject vs between-subject — the decision

```
Does the treatment leave lasting effects (learning, irreversible
intervention, sensitization)?
├─ YES → between-subject (or crossover with verified washout)
└─ NO
   ├─ Is between-unit variability large relative to the effect?
   │   ├─ YES → WITHIN-SUBJECT: each unit is its own control,
   │   │        variance removed via pairing; far fewer subjects
   │   │        needed (sd_diff = sd·√(2(1−r)))
   │   └─ NO → between-subject is simpler and avoids order effects
   └─ Can each unit plausibly complete all conditions
      (burden, time, dropout risk)?
        ├─ NO  → between-subject
        └─ YES → within-subject with counterbalanced order
                 (Latin square; analyze period effects)
```

Within-subject designs are a variance-reduction device: the paired t-test's effective SD shrinks with the within-unit correlation r. But they buy power with assumptions — no carryover, no asymmetric transfer — so state and check them. Mixed designs (some factors within, some between) follow the same logic per factor.

## Control-group design principles

A comparison is only interpretable against the right control. Choose controls by asking *what alternative explanation does this control rule out?*

- **Negative / untreated control** — baseline; separates the treatment effect from spontaneous change over time.
- **Vehicle control** — treatment solvent/delivery medium without active ingredient; separates the active ingredient from the delivery procedure itself.
- **Sham control** — the full procedure minus the key manipulation (e.g. surgery without the lesion); separates the manipulation from the handling/procedure effects.
- **Positive control** — a known-effective condition; proves the assay *can* detect an effect, so a null result is interpretable.
- **Concurrent, not historical** — controls must run in the same batch/time window as treatments, interleaved by randomization. Historical controls confound treatment with time.
- **Blinding where feasible** — allocation concealment and blinded measurement remove observer bias that no statistic can fix later.

## Common pitfalls (structural — cannot be fixed in analysis)

1. **Pseudoreplication.** Treating repeated measurements of one unit as independent replicates: 3 mice with 100 cells measured each is n = 3 (mice), not n = 300, for any treatment applied to the mouse. The replicate must be at the level the treatment is randomized. This single error invalidates a large share of published experiments. (Hurlbert 1984, *Ecological Monographs* 54:187–211.)
2. **Confounding by a nuisance variable.** Running all treatment samples on Monday and all controls on Tuesday confounds treatment with day. Randomize across, or block on, every nuisance factor you can name: batch, day, plate, technician, instrument, position.
3. **No or broken randomization.** Convenience assignment (first-come → treatment) lets confounders sneak in. Use a seeded schedule and follow it.
4. **Missing vehicle/sham control.** Effects attributed to the treatment actually come from the delivery procedure.
5. **Batch effects mistaken for biology.** Never let batch align with condition; interleave conditions across batches.
6. **Edge/position effects.** Plate edges differ (evaporation, thermal gradients). Randomize or block positions; don't put all controls in column 1.
7. **Aliasing ignored.** In a fractional factorial, a "significant main effect" may be an aliased interaction; state the resolution and alias assumptions.
8. **Optimizing with two levels only.** A two-level design can't detect curvature; if an interior optimum matters, plan a follow-up with center points.
9. **Analysis ignoring the design.** Blocks, strata, clusters, and nesting must appear in the model — handing blocked data to a plain t-test throws away the block advantage.

## Review checklist

When reviewing a user's plan, ask:

- What is the experimental unit, and at what level is a true independent replicate?
- Which nuisance factors exist, and is each blocked, stratified, or randomized across?
- Is there a concurrent control of the right kind (negative/vehicle/sham/positive)?
- Is the allocation schedule seeded and archived?
- Does the planned analysis model match the design (blocks, nesting)?
